Broken Garage Door Spring? Call the Genie

A snapped spring is the #1 reason garage doors stop lifting. The door now weighs 250 to 400 pounds with no help from the counterbalance — which is exactly why you don't lift it yourself. Local techs replace springs same-day in most service areas.

Spring Repair in 60 Seconds

  • Two spring types exist: torsion (horizontal bar above the door) and extension (long springs along the side tracks).
  • Springs are rated by cycles, not years. Standard springs last 10,000 cycles — about 7 to 9 years for typical use.
  • If you have two springs and one breaks, replace both. The remaining one is on borrowed time and an unbalanced door wears out everything else.
  • Spring replacement is not a DIY job. Springs hold enough tension to break bones; this is the most common garage door injury.
  • Most spring repairs finish in 60 to 90 minutes once the tech arrives with the correctly rated springs.

Safety First — Read Before You Touch the Door

Do not force a garage door with a broken spring, snapped cable, or off-track section. Garage doors are heavy and can be dangerous when the counterbalance system fails.

  • Don't try to lift the door manually — with the spring gone, you're holding the entire door weight.
  • Don't run the opener — you'll burn out the gear or bend a panel.
  • Don't pull the manual release while the door is up — it can drop fast.
  • Park outside, keep kids and pets clear, and call for service.

The Two Spring Types and Why It Matters

Garage doors use one of two counterbalance systems. The type you have determines the part cost, the labor time, and how often it'll need to be done again.

TypeWhere it sitsTypical lifespanRepair complexity
Torsion spring Horizontal bar mounted above the door header 15,000 to 20,000 cycles (10 to 15 years typical) Higher — high tension, requires winding bars and matched cycle rating
Extension spring Long springs running along each horizontal track 8,000 to 10,000 cycles (7 to 12 years typical) Lower part cost but always paired with safety cables to contain a snap

Most homes built since the early 2000s use torsion springs because they're more durable, smoother to operate, and safer when they fail (the spring stays on the bar instead of flying across the garage). If you have an older door, you may have the side-mounted extension style.

How You Know a Spring Has Failed

Spring failure is one of the most diagnosable problems on a garage door. Look for one or more of these:

  • Loud bang. Often described as a gunshot or a 2x4 hitting concrete. This is the spring snapping under tension.
  • Visible gap. A torsion spring will show a clear 1- to 4-inch gap somewhere along the coil where it broke.
  • Door won't lift, opener strains. Motor hums or grinds, door barely moves or doesn't move at all.
  • Door feels insanely heavy. If you've ever lifted the door manually before, it'll feel two to three times heavier with a broken spring.
  • Door drops fast when released. Without spring tension to hold it, the door has no counterbalance.
  • Door opens crooked or one side hangs. Common when one of two springs breaks — tension is uneven now.

What a Spring Replacement Visit Looks Like

  1. Diagnosis. Tech confirms which spring(s) failed, measures the door weight, counts the cycles needed, and identifies the correct replacement spring rating. Wrong-rated springs are the most common reason a "just replaced" door dies again in 18 months.
  2. Tension release. Any remaining tension in the surviving spring (if applicable) is released safely with winding bars. This is the part homeowners get hurt on.
  3. Spring removal. Old springs come off the shaft. Cables are inspected and replaced if frayed (we usually recommend doing both at once — the labor is already done).
  4. New springs installed. Properly sized and matched. On a two-spring door, both go in even if only one snapped.
  5. Winding and balance. Tech winds the new spring to the correct number of turns, then tests the manual lift. A balanced door lifts halfway and stays there with no drift up or down.
  6. Opener test. Force settings, travel limits, and reverse safety are all verified before the tech leaves.

Why You Should Replace Both Springs Even If Only One Broke

This is the most common pushback from homeowners and one of the most important things we talk through. Here's the math:

  • Both springs were installed at the same time and have the same cycle history.
  • If one just failed, the other is statistically very close to failing — usually within months.
  • An unbalanced door (new spring + old spring) puts uneven tension on cables, drums, and the opener gear. You're trading one repair for several.
  • The labor for replacing one spring vs both is nearly identical. The second spring adds parts cost only.

Which Spring Repair Fits?

If the spring above the door has a visible gap, choose torsion spring replacement. If the springs run along the horizontal tracks, choose extension spring service. The trade-off is matching: the right spring costs more than a generic swap, but it keeps the door balanced and protects the opener.

A broken spring usually announces itself with one sharp bang, then the door feels too heavy to lift or opens only a few inches.

Cycle rating matters when the garage is the main entrance. A standard 10,000-cycle spring fits light use, while 15,000- to 20,000-cycle options fit heavier daily traffic.

A balanced door should stay near halfway open during a manual lift test; if it drops or shoots upward, the spring setup still needs adjustment.

Spring Repair Questions People Ask

How long does the spring last after replacement?

Standard residential springs are rated for about 10,000 cycles — roughly 7 to 9 years for a household that opens the door 4 times a day. Higher-cycle springs (15,000 or 20,000) cost a little more upfront and can last 12 to 15 years; we generally recommend them for any homeowner who uses the garage as the primary entrance.

Is it safe to keep using the door with one broken spring?

No. Even if the door still moves, the opener is now lifting twice the weight it was designed for. You'll burn out the opener gear, stress the cables to the failure point, and risk the door dropping unexpectedly. Stop using it.

Can I just replace the broken spring and leave the other one?

It's possible but not recommended. Most reputable techs will replace both because the second is days or weeks from failing, the door balance will be off if you don't, and the labor is the same. If you genuinely insist on one, most will accommodate — but understand the tradeoff.

How much does spring replacement actually cost?

National ranges run $150 to $400 for a typical residential job, with two-spring jobs trending toward the higher end. Cost depends on door weight, spring type, cycle rating, and whether cables are replaced at the same time. We have a full breakdown on the spring cost page.

How fast can a tech get here?

Most calls placed before noon get same-day service in our active markets. Spring failures are treated as priority dispatch because the door is unusable.

Will the new spring work with my existing opener?

Yes — springs and openers work together but aren't matched parts. The tech will recalibrate the opener's force settings after the new spring is installed so the system runs smoothly.
