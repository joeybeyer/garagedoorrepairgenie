Garage Door Cable Problems?

The thin steel cables that run from the bottom corner of the door to the spring drum carry the door's full weight on every cycle. When one snaps or jumps the drum, the door drops crooked and the system becomes unsafe to use. Don't run the opener — call the Genie.

Cable Repair in 60 Seconds

  • Cables snap or jump for three reasons: rust and wear, a spring failure that shock-loaded them, or the drum loosened on the shaft.
  • Both cables get replaced as a pair. The surviving cable is on the same age and load history as the broken one.
  • Don't run the opener after a cable failure. The remaining cable is overloaded and can snap; panels can bend further.
  • Most cable repairs finish in 60 to 90 minutes and are usually combined with a balance check and roller inspection.
  • Cable replacement is often paired with spring service — the same tension release work has already been done.

Why Broken Cables Are a "Stop and Call" Situation

Cables hold the door against gravity. When one fails, several things happen at once:

  • The door drops on the failed side, often by an inch or two, and the door becomes crooked.
  • The remaining cable is now carrying close to the full weight by itself, well beyond its design load.
  • The bottom panel may be twisted out of alignment with the rollers, putting it at risk of coming off the track entirely.
  • If the spring system is also compromised, the door can fall the rest of the way fast.

Running the opener at this point either snaps the second cable, lets the door wedge crooked in the track, or both. Park outside until the tech arrives.

How to Tell a Cable Has Failed

  • Visible cable hanging loose on one side of the door, often inside the garage near the spring shaft.
  • Door is crooked or one corner is lower than the other.
  • Door makes a loud bang when closing the last few inches as the slack cable lets the door drop.
  • Frayed wire strands visible on a still-intact cable — an early warning that the cable is days from snapping.
  • Door binds or grinds as the rollers sit slightly out of alignment.
  • Tangled cable around the drum after the cable jumped off and wrapped on itself.

What's Actually Happening Inside the System

The cables connect the bottom bracket of the door to the cable drum at the end of the torsion spring shaft (or to the bottom of an extension spring on side-mount systems). When the door opens, the spring unwinds and the cable winds onto the drum. When the door closes, the cable plays back out.

What the Repair Visit Covers

  1. Diagnosis. Tech identifies the failure mode and whether one or both cables need replacement, plus drum and bracket condition.
  2. Tension release. Springs are unwound to take all load off the cables before any work begins.
  3. Cable removal. Old cables come off the drums and the bottom brackets.
  4. Drum inspection. If the drum grooves are stripped or the drum slipped, replacement may be needed.
  5. New cables installed. Properly sized for door height, mated to the drum and bottom bracket.
  6. Spring re-tensioning. Springs wound back to spec.
  7. Balance test. Manual lift — door should hold halfway.
  8. Opener calibration. Force settings retuned to the now-balanced door.

Why Both Cables Get Replaced

Same logic as springs:

  • Both cables were installed at the same time and have run the same number of cycles.
  • The surviving cable has been overloaded since the moment the first one failed.
  • Labor is identical for one cable or two; only the part cost is different.
  • Mismatched cables (one new, one old) make the door uneven and shorten the life of everything else — springs, drums, opener gear.

Cable Repair Questions

How long do garage door cables typically last?

Standard galvanized cables last 8 to 15 years in dry climates, 5 to 10 years in coastal or salt-exposed areas. Stainless cables cost more upfront but last longer in humid environments — worth specifying in places like Savannah, San Antonio, or Vancouver.

Can a frayed cable wait until next week?

If you can see broken strands on the cable, it's days from full failure, not weeks. Once enough strands break, the remaining ones snap one after another. Replacing a frayed cable proactively is a normal-priority service call. Replacing one after it fails is an emergency.

Why do cables fail in coastal areas faster?

Salt air corrodes the steel strands from the outside in. The cable looks fine, then strands start breaking inside the bundle. Stainless steel cables resist this and are a worthwhile upgrade in Savannah, coastal Texas, and humid Pacific Northwest markets.

Does cable replacement require the spring to be replaced too?

Not always — but often it makes sense. The tech is releasing spring tension to do the cable work. Adding spring replacement at that point is a small additional labor charge. If your springs are 7+ years old, it's usually worth doing both at once.

Can a snapped cable damage the door?

Yes. The most common collateral damage is bent panels (from the door dropping crooked) and pulled rollers (from the door wedging in the track). The tech inspects for this on arrival; sometimes the cable is the only fix needed, sometimes additional parts get added to the bill.
