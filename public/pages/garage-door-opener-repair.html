Garage Door Opener Not Working?

Motor humming but the door won't budge? Remote dead, wall button still works? Sensor blinking? Most opener problems trace to one of eight common faults — and most fix in under an hour. Local techs handle belt, chain, screw-drive, and direct-drive openers across all major brands.

Opener Diagnosis at a Glance

  • If the wall button works but the remote doesn't: remote battery, signal interference, or reprogram needed.
  • If neither button works: power supply, wiring, or logic board.
  • If the motor hums but nothing moves: stripped opener gear, broken spring, or jammed track.
  • If the door reverses 6 inches in: photo-eye sensors are misaligned or dirty — often a 5-minute fix.
  • If the opener works intermittently: wiring, receiver board, or RF interference from a nearby device.

Five Things to Try Before You Call

Most homeowners can rule out the simplest causes in 5 minutes. None of these involve the spring, cable, or opener gear — they're all safe.

  1. Replace the remote battery. Even brand-new remotes ship with weak batteries. If the wall button works and the remote doesn't, this is the answer 60% of the time.
  2. Wipe the photo-eye sensors. The two small sensors at the bottom of each track, about 4 inches off the floor. Dust, cobwebs, or a misaligned beam will stop the door from closing. Wipe them with a clean cloth, make sure the indicator lights are solid (not blinking), and try again.
  3. Check the wall outlet. Plug a lamp or phone charger into the same outlet. If it doesn't power up, your breaker may have tripped or the GFCI outlet may need a reset.
  4. Look for the lock button. Many wall consoles have a "lock" or "vacation" button that disables remote control. If a small light is blinking on the wall console, hold the lock button for 5 to 10 seconds to release it.
  5. Check the manual release cord. The red cord hanging from the rail. If it's been pulled, the door is disconnected from the opener trolley. Pull the cord toward the motor (not down) until you hear it click back into the trolley.

If those didn't fix it, the issue is one of the deeper ones below — and a tech is the right call.

Repair vs. Replace — When Each Makes Sense

A common upsell trap is being told you need a whole new opener when you actually just need a part replaced. The honest decision tree:

SituationSmart move
Stripped main drive gear, opener under 10 years oldReplace the gear ($80-$160). Keep the opener.
Logic board failure, opener under 10 years oldReplace the board if available; otherwise consider full replacement
Photo-eye sensor failureReplace the sensors ($40-$80). Keep the opener.
Opener over 15 years old, multiple parts failingFull replacement — new opener will be cheaper than three repairs over two years
Opener older than 1993 (no photo-eye sensors)Full replacement — pre-1993 units don't meet current safety code
Want smartphone control, battery backup, or quiet belt driveReplacement is the only path

Reputable techs will tell you when a part repair makes sense vs. full replacement. If the answer is always "you need a new one," get a second opinion.

What the Tech Checks During an Opener Service Call

  1. Confirm power at the outlet and at the opener motor head
  2. Test wall button, remote, and keypad independently
  3. Inspect photo-eye sensor alignment and indicator lights
  4. Check the manual release trolley engagement
  5. Listen to the motor on a test cycle — humming, grinding, clicking patterns each indicate different faults
  6. Inspect chain or belt tension (loose belts cause shudder)
  7. Test force limits and travel-limit switches
  8. Verify safety reverse on a 1.5-inch obstruction (a 2x4 laid flat under the door)

Opener Questions

Why does my opener hum but the door not move?

Most often the main drive gear has stripped — common on belt and chain drive openers around the 10-year mark. The motor still spins, but the gear that translates motion to the chain or belt is too worn to grip. Less commonly: a broken spring is making the door too heavy for the opener to lift, or the trolley has disengaged from the rail.

My opener was working yesterday and now nothing happens. Did it die?

Probably not. Check the breaker, check the wall outlet (try plugging in a phone charger), check whether the wall lock button has been engaged, and check whether the manual release cord is hanging loose (someone may have pulled it). If those are fine, the wall switch or logic board is the next suspect — both are usually repairable, not a full opener replacement.

How long should an opener last?

Average residential lifespan is 12 to 15 years. Belt drives last a bit longer than chain drives because the belt doesn't wear the gear as fast. Heavy use (primary entrance, multiple cars) shortens the lifespan; a vacation home opener can last 20+ years.

Is it worth upgrading to a smart opener?

Worth it if you want phone control, vacation alerts, package delivery scheduling, or battery backup (required by code in some states for new installs). Not necessary if your existing opener works fine. Many existing openers can be retrofitted with smart-controller add-ons for $40 to $80, avoiding full replacement.

Do all openers come with battery backup?

No. California requires it on new installs (SB 969); most other states don't. If you live somewhere with frequent outages or use the garage as your primary entrance, it's worth specifying when you buy a new opener — the upcharge is small.
