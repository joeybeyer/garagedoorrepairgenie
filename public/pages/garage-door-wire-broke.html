Garage Door Wire Broke? It May Be a Cable Problem

Most homeowners call them "wires." Garage door techs call them "cables." Same part — the thin braided steel lines that run from the bottom of the door to the spring drum. Here's what just happened, what's safe to do, and how the repair actually works.

Quick Answers

  • "Wire" usually means cable. Garage doors don't have load-bearing electrical wires — the things that snap with a bang are steel lift cables.
  • If your door is now crooked or won't lift evenly: a cable broke or jumped its drum.
  • Both cables get replaced as a pair — same age, same load history.
  • Don't run the opener. The remaining cable is overloaded; the door can drop further or pull off track.
  • Most repairs finish in 60 to 90 minutes with same-day dispatch.

Wire vs. Cable: What You're Actually Looking At

Garage doors have a few things that look like wires:

What it looks likeWhat it actually isIf it's broken
Thin braided steel running from the bottom of the door up to the spring shaft Lift cable Door drops, gets crooked, becomes unsafe
Thin two-conductor wire near the bottom of the door tracks, going to the small sensors Photo-eye sensor wire (low voltage) Door won't close, sensors blink — safe to investigate
Wire from the wall button to the opener motor on the ceiling Wall control wire (low voltage) Wall button stops working — remote may still work
Power cord from the opener motor to the ceiling outlet 120V power cord Opener has no power — plug it back in or check the outlet
Long stretched spring along the side track with a thin cable through it Extension spring with safety cable inside Big problem — this is structural, see spring repair

If the door is crooked, won't lift, or made a loud bang — you almost certainly have a cable issue, not an electrical one.

Stop Before You Do Anything Else

If your "wire" snapped and the door is now hanging crooked:

  • Don't run the opener — the second cable is overstressed and can snap.
  • Don't try to lift the door manually — balance is gone.
  • Don't drive under the door, in either direction.
  • Don't pull the manual release while the door is up — gravity wins.

Park outside, keep the kids and pets away from the garage, and call.

What Cables Look Like When They Break

A snapped cable has a distinctive look:

  • One end of the cable is hanging loose, often coiled at the bottom of the door track or wrapped around the drum at the top.
  • The opposite cable looks tight and the door sits crooked — lower on the broken-cable side.
  • Sometimes the cable is still wound on the drum but no longer connected to the door's bottom bracket.
  • Frayed cables show broken individual strands sticking out of the cable bundle — an early-warning sign before full failure.

If you see any of those, you're dealing with cables. Time to call.

Why Cables Snap

  1. Wear and rust over time. Standard galvanized cables last 8 to 15 years in dry climates, less in coastal or humid markets.
  2. Spring failure shock load. When a torsion spring snaps, the cables suddenly absorb the full door weight, often more than they're rated for.
  3. Drum slippage. A loose drum can let the cable wind unevenly until it jumps off and snaps when re-loaded.
  4. Bottom bracket issues. The bracket where the cable attaches to the door can corrode at the connection point, snapping the cable end.
  5. Door off track. A derailed door pulls the cable at strange angles, often jumping it off the drum.
  6. Improper opener force settings. Too-aggressive opener force overstresses the cables on every cycle.

What the Repair Looks Like (the Short Version)

The full repair sequence is on the cable repair page, but the headline is: tech arrives, releases spring tension, swaps both cables (and any damaged drums or brackets), re-winds the springs, balance-tests the door, and recalibrates the opener. Typical visit is 60 to 90 minutes, longer if springs are also being replaced or panels need attention.

Wire/Cable Questions

Is the "wire" you're talking about an electrical wire?

No. The thing that just snapped and made your door crooked is a steel lift cable, not an electrical wire. Garage doors do have low-voltage wiring (for sensors and wall buttons) but those don't bear weight and don't fail with a bang.

Can I splice the broken cable back together?

No. Cables are single-piece steel components engineered to a specific length and tension. Splicing creates a weak point that will fail again, often dangerously. The right answer is replacement of both cables at the same time.

Why do both cables get replaced?

Both cables were installed at the same time, have the same age, and have run the same number of cycles. The surviving cable has been overloaded since the first one failed. Replacing one means the other fails within months — another emergency call.

Will a tech also check the springs?

Yes. Springs and cables work as a system. The tech will check spring tension, look for visible damage, and tell you honestly whether they need replacement now, soon, or are fine. If the cable failed because a spring shock-loaded it, the spring may need attention too.
