What Does Garage Door Spring Replacement Cost?

Honest answer: there is no single number, but there is a real range. Here's what the work actually costs in 2026, what drives the price up or down, and which add-ons are worth saying yes to when the tech is already at your house.

Call for Local Estimate See Price Ranges

The Cost Picture in 60 Seconds

  • Typical national range: $150 to $400 for a residential spring job, with two-spring jobs trending toward the higher end.
  • Service call fees of $75 to $100 are common and are usually waived if you go forward with the repair.
  • Both springs at once costs less than two trips. Labor is the bigger part of the bill; the second spring is mostly part cost.
  • Cycle rating affects price. 10,000-cycle springs are cheapest, 20,000-cycle springs cost more upfront but last roughly twice as long.
  • Pricing varies by market. Big metros run higher; smaller markets cheaper. Emergency calls and after-hours dispatch add a premium.

Pricing can vary based on spring type, door size, number of springs, parts, labor, and local availability. Call for a local estimate.

These are realistic ranges, not quotes. Anyone giving you an exact number sight-unseen is either guessing or planning to renegotiate when they arrive.

What Actually Drives Your Number Up or Down

Six factors decide whether your job lands at the bottom or top of the range above:

  1. Spring count. Single-spring doors are cheaper; two-spring doors run higher. (Most doors built since 2003 have two.)
  2. Door weight. A 7-foot single-car door is light; an insulated 16-foot double-car door is heavy. Heavier doors need bigger, more expensive springs.
  3. Spring type. Torsion springs cost a bit more per part than extension; the labor difference is small.
  4. Cycle rating. 10,000-cycle springs are the standard. 15,000 or 20,000 cost slightly more upfront but last considerably longer.
  5. Location. Major metros run higher than rural markets due to labor costs. Atlanta, Portland, and San Antonio prices are typically mid-range.
  6. Add-ons. Cables, drums, rollers, opener recalibration — all small additions when the tech is already there, but they add to the line items.

Add-Ons Worth Saying Yes To

When the tech has the door open and the tension released, certain extras cost very little incremental labor and meaningfully extend the next service interval:

Replace cables together

Cables and springs work as a pair. Replacing both at once typically costs $40 to $80 more than the spring job alone — and saves a future trip when the cable inevitably wears.

Step up to high-cycle springs

For roughly $50 to $100 more, you get springs rated for 15,000 to 20,000 cycles. On a primary-entrance garage that's an extra 4 to 6 years before the next replacement.

Roller replacement

Standard steel rollers are noisy and wear out. Nylon rollers cost $50 to $100 for a set, run quieter, and last longer. Worth it if you've been hearing screeching during travel.

Opener tune-up

Force settings, travel limits, sensor alignment. After a new spring the opener has to relearn the door balance anyway — a tune-up while the tech is there is usually cheap.

Add-Ons Where You Can Push Back

Not every upsell is worth it. Things you can usually decline without consequence:

  • Full opener replacement when only the gear is bad — gear-only repairs are far cheaper.
  • Track replacement when the existing track is straight and rust-free — most tracks last decades.
  • "Premium" hardware packages bundling things you don't need.
  • Annual maintenance contracts on a door that's working well after the repair — a quick lube once a year is something you can do yourself.

Reputable techs will explain why each line item exists. If they can't, ask why — and feel free to decline.

Why DIY Springs Almost Always End Up Costing More

The math people don't run before they decide to do it themselves:

  • Wrong-spec springs from a hardware store cost $40 to $80 and fail again in 1 to 3 years.
  • Winding bars and torsion tools cost $50 to $80 to buy once.
  • A single misstep can wreck the cables, drums, or panels — often turning a $300 repair into a $700-plus fix.
  • Hospital visits from torsion spring injuries are common enough that ER staff in big cities recognize the wound pattern.

For a $200 to $400 difference, a trained tech with the right springs and the right tools is the cheaper path the vast majority of the time.

Spring Cost Questions

Why don't you publish a flat price?

Because we'd be lying. A garage door spring job depends on door weight, spring count, spring type, cycle rating, parts in stock, market labor rates, and whether cables get replaced too. Anyone publishing a single number is either pricing for the highest-profit scenario or about to surprise you on arrival.

Why is replacing both springs not double the price of one?

Most of the bill is dispatch + labor + tension release + balance test. Those are fixed costs whether the tech replaces one spring or two. The second spring adds its part cost and a few minutes of additional install time. You're typically looking at $40 to $80 more for the second spring.

Are spring kits from Amazon a viable option?

Only if you know your door's exact weight, the wire diameter, the inside diameter, the wound length, and the cycle rating you want. Wrong-spec springs are the most common DIY failure mode. A reputable tech weighs the door first; most kits sold online don't include that step.

Should I get multiple quotes?

For non-emergency repair, yes — two or three quotes will tell you fast if someone's outside the realistic range. For emergency repairs (door stuck open, security risk), you generally take the first qualified tech who can come out.

What if the tech says I need a new opener too?

Sometimes true, sometimes upselling. Ask specifically: "Is the gear stripped, the logic board fried, or just the force settings off?" A gear-only repair is far cheaper than full opener replacement. Demand specifics before agreeing.
