Garage Door Spring Snapped? Do Not Force the Door

If you just heard a loud bang from the garage and the door now feels impossibly heavy, the counterbalance spring failed. The door is unsafe to lift, the opener will damage itself trying, and forcing it makes the repair much more expensive. Stop, step back, and call.

Right-Now Action List

  • Stop trying to lift the door. Without the spring, you're holding 250 to 400 pounds.
  • Stop running the opener. It will burn out the gear or rip the cables.
  • Don't pull the manual release if the door is open — it can drop fast.
  • Park outside if your car is in the garage and you can't safely raise the door.
  • Keep kids and pets clear of the door until a tech arrives.
  • Call for service. Most spring snaps get same-day dispatch.

What Just Happened to Your Door

Garage doors weigh 250 to 400 pounds. The springs above the door (or along the side tracks) hold the door's full weight in stored tension so the opener — or your arms — can lift it easily. When a spring snaps, all that tension releases at once, usually with a noise like a 2x4 hitting concrete or a small gunshot.

Right now, your door has no counterbalance. The opener is rated to lift maybe 30 pounds — not 350. If you keep pressing the wall button, you'll watch one of three things happen: the opener gear strips, the cables snap from being yanked, or the door cracks a panel from being lifted unevenly. Each of those turns a spring repair into a much bigger bill.

How to Confirm It's Actually a Spring

Most "garage door won't open" calls aren't springs — but a snapped spring has very recognizable signatures:

  • You heard the bang. Sharp, loud, often loud enough to wake people up. Sometimes mistaken for a break-in.
  • Visible coil gap. Walk inside the garage (with the door closed). Look up at the horizontal bar above the door, or along the side tracks. A snapped spring shows a clean 1- to 4-inch gap.
  • Door feels enormous. If you can lift it at all, it feels nothing like normal — suddenly twice or three times heavier.
  • Opener strains and stops. Motor hums, door barely moves, opener trips its safety reverse.
  • Door drops fast when pulled. Without spring tension, gravity is winning.

If you see two or more of those, it's a spring. If you only see one, it could still be a cable, opener, or track issue — the tech will sort it out on arrival.

What "Spring Repair Same Day" Actually Looks Like

  1. You call. Describe the bang, the gap (if visible), and your door type if you know it. Dispatch can usually identify the issue from the symptom alone.
  2. Tech is dispatched. Most calls placed before noon get a same-day slot. After-hours calls get a premium rate but get someone there.
  3. Tech arrives, confirms diagnosis. 5 to 10 minutes of inspection: spring count, door weight, cable condition, drum condition.
  4. Quote written. Flat-rate, before any tools come out. You decide if it goes forward.
  5. Repair completed. 60 to 90 minutes for a standard residential door. Both springs replaced if it was a two-spring system.
  6. Balance test & safety test. Door should hold halfway with no drift. Opener force settings recalibrated. You're back in business.

Why "Just Replace the Broken One" Backfires

This is the most common pushback we hear when the tech recommends replacing both springs. The reasoning seems logical — only one is broken, so only one needs replacing. The reality:

  • Both springs were installed at the same time and have the same cycle history. The surviving one is statistically days to months from failing.
  • An unbalanced door (one new spring, one old) puts uneven tension on cables, drums, and the opener — each of which costs more to replace than a spring.
  • Labor is the same whether the tech replaces one spring or two. You're saving the part cost only, while doubling the chance of another emergency call.

What to Tell the Dispatcher

Three pieces of info make the trip faster:

  1. "I heard a bang and the door won't lift." Confirms the symptom.
  2. Door size: single-car (8 to 9 ft wide) or double-car (16 to 18 ft wide).
  3. Approximate door age if you know it — helps the tech bring the right replacement parts.

If you can also confirm whether you have torsion (horizontal bar above) or extension (long springs along the sides), the tech can stock the truck more precisely.

A Quick Human Note

A broken spring is one of those repairs where confidence can get expensive fast. The door may look normal, but the part that carried the weight is no longer doing its job.

If you heard a bang or your door suddenly feels heavy, don't test it three more times. Tell the tech what happened first. That little detail usually matters more than your guess about the brand or model.

Spring Snap Questions

I need my car out and the door is closed — can I lift it manually?

Strongly discouraged. The door now weighs roughly 300 pounds with no counterbalance. Even if you can lift it, the door can fall back down on you, your hands, your car, or your kids. If you absolutely must do it, get at least two adults of comparable size, lift slowly, brace the door open with C-clamps on the track, and don't drive under it — the C-clamps can slip. Better path: park outside until the tech arrives.

Will my homeowner's insurance cover spring replacement?

Almost never. Springs are considered normal wear-and-tear maintenance, like a car alternator or a hot water heater element. The exception is when the spring failed because of an external event like a power surge, a vehicle hitting the door, or a storm event — sometimes those qualify under the personal property or accidental damage clauses. Read your policy or call your agent.

How long does it take to actually fix once the tech arrives?

60 to 90 minutes for a standard residential door. Add 15 to 30 minutes if cables get replaced at the same time. Add more for heavier doors, two-car-wide systems, or doors needing additional repair beyond the spring.

Should I be worried about other parts that might be damaged?

Sometimes a snapping spring whips around and damages cables, drums, or even nearby panels. The tech will inspect for collateral damage on arrival. Most spring snaps are clean failures with no other damage; occasionally something else needs replacing. Either way, you'll know the full picture before any work starts.

Is the spring failure a sign the door is too old?

Not necessarily. Springs are wear items with a finite cycle life regardless of door age. A 5-year-old door used as the primary entrance can hit end-of-life on its 10,000-cycle springs. A 25-year-old door used twice a week may still be on the originals. The door panels, tracks, and opener can long outlive the springs.
