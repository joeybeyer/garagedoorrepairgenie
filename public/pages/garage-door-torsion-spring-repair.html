Torsion Spring Problems? Genie Can Help

The horizontal bar above your garage door holds enough stored energy to break bones — which is exactly why torsion spring work is a tech-only repair. Local pros replace yours the same day, in matched pairs, with the correct cycle rating for your door weight.

What to Know Before the Tech Arrives

  • A torsion spring is the horizontal coil mounted on a steel shaft above your door header — if you don't see long springs along the side tracks, you have torsion.
  • A snap is usually visible: a 1- to 4-inch gap somewhere along the coil, sometimes with a sharp twist where it broke.
  • Two-spring systems should be replaced as a pair, even when only one snapped — cycle history matches, so the second is close behind.
  • Cycle rating matters more than price — the right spring sized for your door weight is what determines how long the next one lasts.
  • Do not attempt to wind or unwind a torsion spring with regular tools. Winding bars are specialized for a reason.

Why This Is the One Repair We Always Say "Don't DIY"

Torsion springs hold roughly 200 to 400 pounds of stored energy in the wound coil. When that energy releases unexpectedly — from a slipped winding bar, a wrong-sized spring, or improper unwinding — it goes somewhere fast. ER doctors see torsion spring injuries every week.

Specifically:

  • Don't try to lift the door manually with a snapped spring.
  • Don't run the opener; it'll burn out trying to lift the unsupported door weight.
  • Don't pull the manual release while the door is up.
  • Don't attempt to re-wind a partially intact spring with hand tools.

How to Tell If You Have a Torsion Spring

Stand inside the garage with the door closed and look up at the wall above the door opening:

What you seeSpring type
One or two coils on a horizontal steel bar across the header Torsion spring
Long stretched springs running parallel to the side tracks (left and right) Extension spring
No visible springs at all Either a hidden side-mount torsion or an EZ-set system — tech can confirm

Most garage doors built since 2003 use torsion. Heavier doors (insulated, wood, oversized) almost always do because torsion handles the weight better.

Signs Your Torsion Spring Is Failing or Already Failed

  • A loud bang from the garage, often mistaken for a gunshot or a panel falling
  • A visible gap or twist in the coil where the metal separated
  • The opener strains, hums, or trips its safety reverse without lifting the door
  • The door feels enormously heavy when you try to lift it manually
  • The door slams the last few inches when closing because the coil has lost tension
  • Squealing or pinging noises from the spring shaft when the door cycles
  • Visible rust, gaps, or coil separation on the spring itself during a routine inspection

What Determines the Right Replacement Spring

Three numbers must match the door, not just the old spring:

  1. Wire diameter — the thickness of the steel wire that forms the coil. Measured in thousandths of an inch.
  2. Inside diameter — the inner measurement of the coil itself, usually 1¾", 2", or 2¼".
  3. Wound length — the spring's compressed length, calculated from door weight and lift height.

A reputable tech weighs the door (or uses the manufacturer's weight chart) and matches the spring to the actual load. Wrong-sized springs are the #1 reason a door "just had springs replaced" needs them done again two years later. Ask your tech what cycle rating they're installing — 10,000, 15,000, or 20,000 — and what door weight it was sized for.

Cycle Ratings Translated to Real Years

"Cycle" means one full open-close. Most homes do 4 to 6 cycles per day if the garage is the primary entrance, fewer if it's just for cars.

What the Replacement Visit Includes

  1. Door weight measurement and cycle-rating selection
  2. Tension release on any surviving springs using winding bars
  3. Old spring(s) removed from the shaft
  4. Cable inspection (and replacement if frayed — usually recommended while the shaft is open)
  5. New springs installed in matched pairs
  6. Winding to manufacturer-spec turn count
  7. Manual lift balance test — door should hold halfway with no drift
  8. Opener force-setting recalibration and safety-reverse test

Total visit time: 60 to 90 minutes for a standard residential door. Heavier doors, two-car-wide doors, or those needing additional repairs (cables, drums, rollers) take longer.

A Quick Human Note

A broken spring is one of those repairs where confidence can get expensive fast. The door may look normal, but the part that carried the weight is no longer doing its job.

If you heard a bang or your door suddenly feels heavy, don't test it three more times. Tell the tech what happened first. That little detail usually matters more than your guess about the brand or model.

Torsion Spring Questions

Can I replace a torsion spring myself with the right tools?

Technically possible. Practically discouraged. The tools are inexpensive but the procedure has zero margin for error: wrong cycle counts cause re-failure, slipped winding bars cause hand and arm injuries, and improper sizing wrecks the cables and drums. Most homeowners who try it end up calling a tech anyway, after spending money on the wrong-spec springs.

How long does the replacement actually last?

That depends on the cycle rating of the spring installed and how often you cycle the door. A 10,000-cycle spring on a primary-entrance garage lasts about 7 years; a 20,000-cycle spring lasts about 13. The math is on the cycle table above.

Why does my door have one spring on some doors and two on others?

Single-spring systems work on lighter doors (single-car, uninsulated). Two-spring systems are standard on heavier double doors, insulated doors, and wood doors. If you have two and one breaks, both get replaced — the surviving spring is on borrowed time.

What if my opener was struggling before the spring broke?

That's actually a tell. Openers strain when the spring is losing tension. If your opener has been working harder, your spring was failing — and the gear inside the opener may need to be checked too. The tech can do both during the same visit.

Are torsion springs more expensive than extension?

Slightly, on parts. The labor is similar. But torsion typically lasts longer, runs quieter, and is safer when it fails (the spring stays on the bar). For most homeowners the upgrade pays back.
